Hi, I'm Scott Kirk, Broker and Owner of Weichert, Realtors - Sierra Pacific Group in Folsom. If you're watching this, you may have just lost a parent, and now there's a house. Maybe it's the one you grew up in. People call me in that first month and they're not asking about commission. They're asking, what do I do first, and am I even allowed to sell it.

So I built a site that answers the four questions every Folsom estate has to answer, in the order you'll hit them, with the California rule and a link to the source for each one. Let's walk through it. I'll be clear about what's my job and what belongs to your attorney and your CPA. Four numbers set the stage. $750,000: if the house was the main home and is worth up to that, there may be a simpler court path than full probate, for a death on or after April 1, 2025.

Four months: the minimum creditor claim period in a full probate. 15 days: the notice heirs get before a sale under full authority. And $1,044,586: how much added value a child who moves in can keep the parent's low property tax base on under Prop 19, through February 15, 2027. We'll come back to each one. Question one.

Who has the right to sell it? That depends on how the house was held. If it was in a living trust, the trustee can sell it, usually without court. If there was a will but no trust, the executor needs the court to appoint them first. And once appointed, the executor has either full authority or limited authority under California's Independent Administration of Estates Act.

That one word changes everything about how the sale runs. With full authority, the executor can usually sell without a hearing. They send the heirs a Notice of Proposed Action at least 15 days before, and if nobody objects, the sale closes like a normal one. With limited authority, the sale has to be confirmed by the court. That means an appraisal by the court's probate referee, a price of at least 90 percent of that appraisal, and a hearing where other buyers can show up and outbid your buyer.

Here's how the overbid works, because buyers need to know this going in. Say the accepted offer is $650,000. The first overbid at the hearing has to be at least 10 percent more on the first $10,000 and 5 percent more on the rest. That's $1,000 plus $32,000, so $683,000. I tell buyers up front how the hearing works, I price against the referee's appraisal, and I keep backup buyers in touch in case there's an overbid.

Your probate attorney runs the hearing. I run the marketing so the house arrives at that hearing with a strong buyer and a clean file. And if one heir objects to the notice? Under full authority, the executor can't go ahead alone as proposed. It may need the court's supervision or instructions.

That's a question for the attorney, not for me. But I'll say this from experience: most of the time the objection is really about price, and a real appraisal and real comparable sales settle it faster than an argument. Question two. Does it go through probate at all? Start at the top of this flow.

Was the house in a living trust? Yes: the trustee sells it, no probate court, usually the fastest path. No: was it the main home, worth $750,000 or less, and did the person die on or after April 1, 2025? If yes, California has a simpler main home petition. You still wait 40 days and the probate referee still appraises it, but it's lighter than full probate.

Above $750,000, or not the main home, it's full probate in the superior court of the county where the person lived. For a Folsom resident that's Sacramento County. Where does Folsom land on that $750,000 line? This past year, 359 of Folsom's 762 single-family sales closed at $750,000 or less. About 47 percent.

So nearly half of Folsom houses could fall under the main home petition, if the other conditions are met. Your attorney confirms whether the estate qualifies. A neighborhood median can't decide it. But it tells you the question is worth asking. How long does full probate take?

A few steps have minimum times set by law. Creditors get at least four months after the executor is appointed to file claims. Heirs get at least 15 days notice before a sale under full authority. The whole thing usually runs longer than the minimums, because courts have calendars and estates have surprises. The page lays out the timeline step by step with the Probate Code section for each.

Question three. What is it worth? An estate usually needs two values, sometimes three. First, the date of death value. That's what the house was worth the day the person died, and it sets the tax basis for the heirs.

In a probate, the court's probate referee sets it. Outside probate, a licensed appraiser does. Second, today's market value, which is what we list it at. And third, if the sale has to be court confirmed, the confirmation appraisal, dated within a year of the hearing, that the price has to clear at 90 percent. This map shows the median sale price by Folsom neighborhood over the past twelve months.

Hillcrest and Lakeview Oaks are over $1.5 million. The group of smaller neighborhoods outside the named ones, where a lot of inherited homes sit because they were built in the 70s and 80s, had a median of $635,000. A median is a starting point, not a value. An original 1979 kitchen and a remodeled one on the same street are not the same house. Everyone asks: do trust and probate houses sell for less?

Sometimes, and it's usually not because of the word probate. It's because the house is dated, it sat empty, or the heirs disagreed on price and it sat on the market. The fix is preparation. Clear it, clean it, fix the small things, price it against real sales, and disclose what you know. A prepared estate house sells like any other house.

The page shows the comparison from the sales data. Question four. What about the taxes? Two separate tax questions, and people mix them up. First, capital gains.

When you inherit, the tax basis of the house steps up to its value at the date of death. If Mom bought it for $90,000 in 1985 and it was worth $700,000 when she passed, your basis is $700,000, not $90,000. Sell it for $720,000 and the gain is $20,000, not $630,000. That's federal law, and your CPA confirms how it applies to you. Second, property tax, and this is where Prop 19 comes in.

If a child inherits the parent's main home and makes it their own main home, and files for the homeowners' exemption within a year, the home can pass without a full reassessment, up to a cap. Right now that cap is the parent's taxable value plus $1,044,586, and that figure is good through February 15, 2027. If you keep it as a rental or a second home, expect it to be reassessed to today's value. The claim goes to the Sacramento County Assessor. Again, confirm it with your CPA.

Which brings up the real question: keep it, rent it, or sell it? Keep it and live in it: Prop 19 may protect the tax base. Rent it: the property tax resets, you become a landlord, and you still have siblings to settle with. Sell it: the step up in basis means little or no capital gains tax, and everyone gets their share. There's no right answer.
